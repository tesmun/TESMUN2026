SEVEN COMMITTEES, ONE STRIKE, ONE MISSION: TESMUN 2026 INAUGURATED
By The Editorial Board (TESMUN PRESS)
TESMUN 2026 was officially inaugurated on Tuesday, September 1, opening with a call for delegates to look beyond school walls and confront pressing global issues. Coinciding with Ganesh Chaturthi, a festival symbolizing new beginnings and wisdom, the ceremony set an ambitious tone for the days ahead.
In his opening address, Secretary-General Dibas Khadka called on delegates to tackle pressing global challenges, including recent climate-driven glacial flooding. "Security doesn’t mean more weapons," Khadka said. "The world already has spectators, what it needs is participants. Challenge ideas, not people; speak boldly, but leave room for others' thoughts."
Addressing the assembly during Ganesh Chaturthi, Principal Prasant Rajak cited the story of Lord Ganesha and Ved Vyasa to emphasize critical thinking and deep comprehension in international affairs. "Nepal is not separate from world problems; our Himalayan terrain makes us particularly vulnerable. Listen deeply, think critically, and speak wisely. You are rehearsing for the real world."
As the applause died down, MCs Pari Maharjan and Sofia Roka introduced the badge distribution segment, with MUN coordinator Govinda Gautam presenting badges across the dais, delegates, and committee heads to mark each member's role for the session.
The ceremony's most prestigious segment followed, as Managing Director Ram Basnet presented gavels to the executive secretariat and committee chairs, formally opening deliberations across seven committees.
Anticipation peaked as the lights dimmed and the curtains parted for the opening video, prepared by the IT team. The hall responded with bursts of cheers and applause with each climactic scene.
With the loud bang of the gavel by the Secretary General, the ceremony dissolved, and every delegate stood up to officially commence the event. The students stepped into their respective committees as the 14th session of TESMUN kicked off into full swing.

QUESTIONS GO UNANSWERED AS DELEGATES CLASH OVER NEPAL’S TRAFFICKING RESPONSE!
By Krishna Shrestha and Aarush Kattuwal, Chief Reporters of MP-I
Kathmandu, 1 September
The TES MUN Human Rights Council session on Protecting Human Rights in Nepal focused on government action to combat human trafficking and child abuse. While all twelve delegates delivered their General Speakers List speeches, the real action unfolded during the Q&A rounds as delegates put each other's policies to the test.
Sanish Karki, representing Suhang Nembang, questioned Shivatmika Bhatta, representing Rabi Lamichhane, during his GSL speech. Karki also raised a question during the GSL speech of Prinsha Shrestha, representing Swornim Wagle.
During the GSL of the delegate representing Harka Raj Rai, questions were raised by the delegates representing Balendra Shah and Rabi Lamichhane. The questions were not answered during the session. Bikram Timilsina, represented by Suprim Subedi, also responded to a question during the same GSL.
Further exchanges took place during Aambika Devi Sangraula’s GSL, when she questioned the delegate representing Mohammad Istiyak Rai. Later, Bikram Timilsina was questioned by Ain Bahadur Mahar, represented by Pema Yangzom.
Questions were also answered by delegates other than those initially addressed. During Bhaswan Man Pradhan’s GSL, a question directed toward the delegate representing Gyan Bahadur Shahi, Siddhima Kansakar, was answered by the delegate representing Rabi Lamichhane instead.
The twelve scheduled GSL speeches were completed by the end of the session. The subsequent Q&A rounds covered government measures, accountability, human trafficking, child abuse and the positions of individual political representatives. The committee is expected to continue discussions on trafficking and child protection.

DELEGATES CLASH OVER INDEPENDENCE OF ANTI-CORRUPTION INVESTIGATIONS!
By Pari Maharjan, Chief Reporter of MP-II
1 September 2026
Delegates in Legislative Parliament II questioned the independence of Nepal’s anti-corruption institutions, debating whether political influence could affect investigations involving former officials and individuals connected to political leaders.
The discussion focused on political interference, institutional safeguards and accountability within political parties. Rabi Lamichhane’s representative, Prasanna Puri, questioned whether the CIAA had sufficient mechanisms to prevent political pressure from influencing investigations.
Manjul Pratap Ghimire, representing Balendra Shah, argued that corruption investigations should be conducted consistently and without political selectivity. Suhang Nembang’s representative, Somiya Gupta, acknowledged the concerns but argued that allegations involving individual members should not characterise an entire political party as corrupt.
The discussion also addressed alleged financial losses involving Nepal Airlines, including a reported loss of Rs. 1.47 million. Delegates called for greater transparency and questioned how the reported losses would be recovered.
Before delegates could conclude the session, Secretary-General Dibas Khadka criticized the depth of the debate. Shortly after his remarks, committee proceedings were abruptly interrupted when a crisis alarm sounded.

NO PARTY SPARED! CORRUPTION DOMINATES THE GSL!
By Aashiya Shrestha, Shambhavi Lama and Sampanna Pachhai, Reporters of MP-III
1 September 2026
Party promises faced a tough test in Model Parliament III as delegates fired questions on corruption, government responsibility and past records, turning the GSL into a fast-moving debate circling around accountability.
Uplabdhi Bartaula, representing Rabi Lamichhane, raised concern over the Pokhara Airport project’s alleged Rs. 8.36 billion cost inflation. Divya Pudasaini, representing Suhang Nembang, also brought up CIAA asset probes involving personal secretaries of senior UML leaders.
Anjas Khanal, representing Gyan Bahadur Shahi, declared that RPP would listen to citizens and strengthen its anti-corruption efforts. Sulav Ghimire, representing Shishir Khanal, raised allegations that donations collected after the Bhotekoshi River flood had been misused for personal accounts.
Nepal Congress also came under question, with Pallabi Neupane challenging the party’s manifesto and past record. By the end of the GSL, delegates had put every party’s promises under interrogation, but debates over accountability were far from over.

CHEMICAL WEAPONS, DIPLOMATIC WAR TENSIONS! DISEC DIVIDES!
By Osang Ghising and Shubham Shrestha, Chief Reporters of DISEC
Kathmandu, 1 September
The third session of the TESMUN Disarmament and International Security Committee brought renewed debate over nuclear, chemical, biological and conventional weapons, with delegates divided over disarmament, national security and foreign military intervention.
The United States delegation, represented by Aashima Bhattarai, highlighted the destructive consequences of nuclear weapons and later accused Russia of maintaining covert chemical-weapons capabilities. Russia, represented by Sabhya Devkota, rejected the allegations and accused Ukraine of using toxic chemical weapons since February 2023. Ukraine denied the allegations and cited records from the OPCW.
Japan stated that it does not possess, store, or seek to acquire nuclear weapons, while Belarus rejected claims concerning chemical capabilities on its territory. The committee moved into a Moderated Caucus on strengthening disarmament measures for nuclear, drone, chemical and biological weapons.
The session concluded with delegates continuing to debate disarmament measures, the responsibilities of states possessing weapons capabilities and the limits of foreign military intervention.

GSL HEATS UP OVER TRAFFICKING AND CHILD PROTECTION!
By Anishka Kuikel and Sambridhi Phuyal, Chief Reporters of HRC
1 September 2026
Delegates in the Human Rights Council confronted member states over systemic trafficking, forced labor, and child exploitation as TESMUN XIV’s session opened.
The United States questioned Indonesia regarding child labor in farming sectors. The delegate of Indonesia detailed national policy initiatives, though delegates continued to press Indonesia on recruitment practices and systemic exploitation of female migrant workers abroad.
The floor grew contentious during Syria’s address on trafficking vulnerabilities. Geopolitical tensions escalated during Russia’s GSL regarding accusations surrounding forced child transfers, while representatives of the United States and United Kingdom demanded to know why transferred children had not yet been repatriated.
Economic displacement and child welfare were highlighted when the United States questioned Afghanistan about conditions that could lead a poor father to sell his daughter. Proceedings were suspended following a committee crisis.

FUEL ON FIRE: ECOSOC CLASHES OVER WHO PAYS FOR ENERGY CRISIS
By Shreni Chapagain and Alishka Kuikel, Chief Reporters of ECOSOC
1 September 2026
Delegates in ECOSOC confronted the global fuel and energy crisis, debating strategies to keep energy affordable and accessible while reinforcing supply chains against disruption.
Ethiopia’s delegate challenged the United States over energy export policies. The U.S. delegation defended exports as vital for revenue and international development. South Africa redirected scrutiny toward South Korea over internal energy shortages, while South Korea defended its transition toward renewable energy.
Brazil linked U.S. policy to Middle East instability, while Iran shifted the focus to the war in Eastern Europe and Ukraine’s role in energy market shocks. Ukraine rejected the framing and emphasized diverse supply chains and affordability.
As the session closed, delegates prepared for the upcoming moderated caucus, but questions remained on energy security and supply chain resilience.

UNEP DEBATES ENVIRONMENTAL COST OF GLOBAL AI EXPANSION
By Ranish Mahat, Chief Reporter of UNEP
Kathmandu, 1 September
The United Nations Environment Programme debated the environmental impact of expanding artificial intelligence infrastructure, focusing on data-center water consumption, electronic waste, and dependence on non-renewable energy.
The discussion intensified when Nepal questioned the United States over the environmental impact of data centers, citing a facility in Georgia that reportedly used 30 million gallons of water. The United States acknowledged the challenges and pointed to efforts to reduce water consumption and improve sustainability.
The debate expanded to the energy requirements of AI infrastructure. China questioned Iran over sustainable AI development while operating data infrastructure reliant on natural gas, and questioned the United Arab Emirates regarding fossil-fuel reliance and digital-infrastructure waste.
Germany proposed requirements for data centers to transition toward renewable energy and replace drinking water in cooling systems with treated wastewater. Russia and the Philippines advocated greater use of hydropower and renewable energy, while Afghanistan raised concerns over insufficient regulation.
The discussion concluded with delegates divided over whether international regulations governing data-center energy and water use should be binding.
