OPENING SPEECH (in bold, bigger size)


Thank you Pari and Sofiya. Thank you very much.

Good morning to our distinguished Guests, Dear Teachers, Secretariat, Executive Board, and Delegates,

I extend my deepest respect to our Chief Guest, honourable Managing Director Mr. Ram Basnet, whose vision paves our way, our honourable Special Guest, Chairperson Madam Sheetal Rana, the guardian of our school family, our respected Guest, Principal Mr. Prashant Rajak, our respected Administrative Incharge, Mr. Neeraj Bikram Rana, and our chy Tellow dais panel members, press and IT teams, Logistic naan our respected feather our MUN Advisor, Mr. Govinda Gautam, my managers, and dear delegates,

I stand before you today as your representative, your very own Secretary General and I am delighted to welcome you to this opening session. I am here today as the torch bearer of our tradition following the legacy that our forerunners have paved on. We are starting our grand Model United Nations Sessions here.

But before we begin, I want you to look beyond this room.

Look at Nepal.

Just days ago, our country witnessed devastating floods triggered by a glacial collapse. Lives were lost, families were displaced, roads and bridges destroyed, and communities left searching for their loved ones.

Now look beyond our borders.

There are wars where civilians pay the price. There are families struggling with rising energy costs. Women and children are being trafficked and exploited. And as technology races forward, even something as promising as artificial intelligence brings new questions about energy, water and waste.

These may sound like problems for presidents, diplomats and ministers.

But they are not. They are problems for humanity.

And humanity begins with us.

So what kind of world do I envision?

I envision a world where security does not mean having more weapons, but having fewer reasons to use them.

A world where energy is affordable, development is sustainable, technology serves people rather than exhausting our planet, and no woman or child is treated as a commodity.

A world where governments are accountable, public money is protected, corruption is challenged, and democracy means more than simply casting a vote.

And above all, I envision a world where a person's birthplace does not decide the value of their life.

But a vision means nothing if it remains a speech.

So what will we do about it?

We will debate.

In DISEC, you will ask: How do we make the world safer when weapons are becoming more powerful and technology is becoming more dangerous?

In ECOSOC, you will ask: How do we keep energy affordable while keeping economies stable? 
In UNEP, you will confront a question of our generation: Can technological progress exist without destroying the resources that sustain us?

In HRC, you will speak for women and children whose voices have been silenced by trafficking, exploitation and war.

And in our Model Parliament, you will bring these questions home confronting human trafficking, child abuse, corruption, misuse of public funds, political opportunism and the need for genuine democratic accountability in Nepal.

But delegates, remember this:

You are not here simply to win a debate. You are here to build an answer.

A good speech may impress a room.

A good argument may win a vote.

But a good resolution can change the direction of a conversation.

So challenge ideas, not people.

Disagree, but listen. Negotiate, but do not surrender your principles. Speak boldly, but leave room for another voice. Because diplomacy is not about finding people who agree with you. Diplomacy is about finding a way forward with people who don't.

Today, these chambers are simulations. Tomorrow, some of you may sit in real parliaments, laboratories, courts, businesses, classrooms and international organisations.The placards you raise today may become the policies you shape tomorrow. So, delegates, be curious enough to question, courageous enough to speak, humble enough to listen, and compassionate enough to remember the human being behind every agenda.
The world has enough spectators. What it needs are participants.And for the next few days, that is exactly who you are.The world is not perfect. Nepal is not perfect. And neither are we.
But perhaps that is where change begins not by pretending everything is fine, but by having the courage to say:

"We can do better."

THANK YOU

With immense pride, hope and excitement, I officially declare TESMUN 2026 OPEN!

The floor is yours.

The future is yours. And the time to begin is now.


CLOSING SPEECH (in bold, bigger size)

"Speak up, speak out, get in the way. When you see something that is not right, not fair, not just, you have a moral obligation to do something." American House of Representative Member and social activist John Lewis once said.
Thank you to our hosts, Rishika and Lisha.
Good morning Chief Guest of this closing session and honorable Managing Director Mr. Ram Basnet, whose vision paves our way, 
our honorable Special Guest and the guardian of our school family Chairperson Madam Sheetal Rana, 
our respected Guest and Principal Mr. Prashant Rajak, our respected Administrative Incharge, Mr. Neeraj Bickram Rana, and our MUN Advisor Mr. Govinda Gautam, my fellow dais panel members, press and IT teams, Logistic managers, and dear delegates, 
We did not gather here over these past days simply to suit up in formal attire. We did not come merely to strike a gavel. We came because history is not written by the silent. History is written by the vocalist. From the ancient councils of philosophy to the modern chambers of the United Nations, progress has always been born and will always be born from the friction of debate. When systems break down, when inequality deepens, when our climate fractures, the world does not need more silence. The world needs sound. The world does not need self-satisfaction. The world needs conviction. Model United Nations is not just a simulation of diplomacy; it was your ultimate rehearsal for changing the world.
My journey on this floor began not with a title or a microphone, but in the quiet corners of Grade 7, as a typist—hours spent translating heated debates into text, earning the Most Hardworking Typist award. I was the scribe before the speaker, the listener before the leader; but, as they say, the fire of debate catches quickly.
In Grade 8, I stepped onto MP-2 as KP Sharma Oli of the CPN-UML and earned Outstanding Delegate. In Grade 9, I crossed borders into UNEP as the Russian Federation, securing another Outstanding Delegate. At RBSMUN, I entered the House of Representatives as Nepal’s Foreign Minister from the Nepali Congress, earning a Special Mention.
I share this not to recount my past, but to illuminate your potential: I was a typist; I became a delegate; a delegate became a diplomat; and today, Secretary-General.
That was all past and personal. Let me share some recent memories made in the hall. How could I forget the delegate of Harka Raj Rai from MP-3 sharing his views dramatically though suspended, the formation of the high-level tribunal for investigating a corruption case against a delegate in MP-2, taking shadow delegates in committee and igniting them to come up with ideas. 
In UNEP, the Hague came home, and Iranian delegates vehemently defended the side. In ECOSOC, the funny moment came when a delegate stole oil from Iran during the crisis and suggested using mustard oil instead of fossil fuel. The presidential speech in DISEC was unforgettable as a US delegate came front. HRC deported the US president for his human rights cases during the crisis created a really funny moment. Crazy things happened. These all were possible. Dear delegate, you made it. Congratulations for standing your ground. Congratulations for daring to speak. Over these days, you did not just represent nations—you carried the weight of the world on your desks. Watching each committee unfold was nothing short of extraordinary. Your resolutions have been carefully carved into craving stories by the press crew. You may visit the wide and wonderful webpages and see them with eyes wide open. 
Dear all, you did not let fear keep your placards down. 
You did not come here to tear down opponents with rhetoric; you came here to lift up humanity with resolutions. You debated with conviction. You negotiated with grace. You led with a fierce, unyielding empathy. When you spoke, you spoke as if the future depended on it and because you spoke, you proved that yes, we can forge a better accord; yes, we can bridge our deepest divides.
To the award winners tonight: let your recognition be a reminder of your potential, not the peak of it. And to those who do not walk away with one: remember that the true measure of a diplomat is not the certificate in your hand, but the courage in your chest. Every delegate who raised a placard, every delegate who drafted a clause, every delegate who dared to speak has won a victory over silence.
The committee sessions may be over, but the work of a diplomat never ends. The placards you put down today are the promises you carry into tomorrow.
The placards have fallen, the gavels are silent, but the legacy begins now so go forth and change the world. Thank you!
